The Hedonic Treadmill: Why Success Never Feels Like Enough

You get the raise, the bigger apartment, the promotion — and within weeks it feels normal again. This isn't a personal failing. It's one of the most well-replicated findings in happiness research.

In 1978, psychologists Philip Brickman, Dan Coates, and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman published a study that has shaped happiness research ever since. They compared the self-reported well-being of recent lottery winners against a control group and, in a separate comparison, against people who had recently become paraplegic following an accident. The lottery winners were not dramatically happier than the control group. More strikingly, the difference in well-being between lottery winners and accident victims, while present, was far smaller than intuition would predict — and both groups, when asked to rate the pleasure they took in ordinary daily activities, had adapted substantially toward their pre-event baseline. This finding, replicated and refined many times since with better methodology, is the empirical foundation of what's called the hedonic treadmill: the tendency of human well-being to return to a relatively stable baseline regardless of major positive or negative life events.

How the Treadmill Actually Works

The mechanism is adaptation, and it is not a psychological flaw — it is a feature of perception that operates on nearly every dimension of experience, not just happiness. The same neural process that makes a loud room feel quiet after twenty minutes, or a strong smell fade from awareness, applies to circumstances: a raise that initially feels transformative becomes the new normal against which subsequent income is judged, typically within a few months. This is sometimes called "hedonic adaptation," and later researchers, including Sonja Lyubomirsky, refined the original treadmill concept by showing that adaptation isn't uniform — some things (income, material possessions, physical comfort) are adapted to quickly and almost completely, while others (chronic noise, long commutes, caregiving strain) are adapted to slowly or only partially.

The asymmetry matters enormously for how you should actually plan your life. If you assume all life changes will produce a permanent boost or permanent hit to your happiness, you will consistently overinvest in things that produce only a temporary bump (a bigger house, a new gadget) and underweight the things that produce genuinely lasting drag (a long commute, a chronically stressful relationship, ambient noise) because you falsely assume you'll adapt to those too. Research on commuting specifically is one of the more striking findings here: long commutes are one of the few circumstances people reliably fail to adapt to, and commute length shows up repeatedly as a strong negative predictor of daily well-being, even years into the same commute.

Adaptation is not the enemy of happiness. Failing to plan around which things you adapt to — and which you don't — is.

Why This Isn't an Argument for Giving Up on Goals

It would be easy to read the hedonic treadmill research as evidence that ambition is pointless — that nothing you achieve will ever feel like enough, so why bother striving. That is not what the research supports. Achievement and improvement still matter, for reasons the treadmill framework doesn't dispute: mastery, purpose, relationships, and contribution all show more durable effects on well-being than status or material acquisition specifically. What the research argues against is a narrower and more common mistake: expecting a specific external achievement to produce lasting emotional payoff on its own, then feeling confused or like a failure when the payoff fades on schedule.

The treadmill explains, for instance, why the pursuit of goals for their own sake — the process, the mastery, the progress — tends to produce more durable satisfaction than achieving any single milestone. Our piece on the progress principle covers the related research showing that the daily experience of making progress, not the achievement of a final outcome, is what actually predicts sustained motivation and well-being.

What Actually Slows Adaptation

Happiness researchers have identified several strategies that measurably slow hedonic adaptation, meaning the boost from a positive change lasts longer than it otherwise would. None of these prevent adaptation entirely — that isn't realistic — but each extends the window before a positive circumstance fades into the new normal.

  • Variety over habituation: varied positive experiences (different restaurants, changing routines, novel activities) are adapted to more slowly than a single repeated luxury, because novelty itself is part of what generates the positive response.
  • Savoring and active appreciation: deliberately noticing and reflecting on a positive circumstance — rather than letting it fade into background routine — measurably slows adaptation. This is the same mechanism behind why a structured gratitude practice produces durable well-being gains: it forces re-noticing of things that would otherwise be adapted to and taken for granted.
  • Experiences over possessions: a substantial body of research, much of it from Thomas Gilovich, finds that money spent on experiences produces more lasting satisfaction than money spent on material goods, in part because experiences become part of identity and memory in a way that resists the comparison-based adaptation that possessions invite.
  • Spending on others: pro-social spending — using resources to benefit other people rather than yourself — shows less hedonic adaptation than self-directed spending in multiple experimental studies, possibly because it connects to a stable source of meaning (contribution, relationships) rather than a comparison-based one (status, possessions).

Applying This to Ambition and Career

Planning Around the Treadmill

  • Weight quickly-adapted-to factors lower when making major life decisions: salary increases, house size, and material upgrades tend to produce smaller, shorter-lived well-being gains than they appear to promise in advance.
  • Weight slowly-adapted-to factors higher: commute time, autonomy, relationship quality, and chronic stress levels have outsized and durable effects on day-to-day well-being — and deserve more weight in decisions than their "impressiveness" alone would suggest.
  • Anchor satisfaction in process, not outcome: because outcomes are adapted to quickly, building identity and satisfaction around the process of skill-building, contribution, and relationships produces a more stable baseline than chasing the next achievement.
  • Notice deliberately. Building a habit of savoring — even briefly acknowledging a good circumstance rather than letting it become invisible background — is one of the few interventions shown to genuinely slow adaptation rather than just changing what you compare yourself to. See our piece on scarcity and abundance for the related mindset shift.

None of this requires abandoning ambition or material comfort — it requires calibrating expectations correctly. A raise is genuinely good and worth pursuing; it is just not going to produce the six-month happiness boost your imagination promises when you're negotiating for it. Knowing that in advance changes how much weight you should put on any single external win versus the more durable factors — autonomy, relationships, purpose, and process — that the research consistently shows hold up better over time. Key Takeaways

The Hedonic Treadmill at a Glance

  • Well-being tends to return to a relatively stable baseline after both major positive and negative events — this is hedonic adaptation, first demonstrated in the 1978 lottery-winner study.
  • Adaptation is not uniform: material gains and status changes fade quickly, while chronic stressors like long commutes or relationship strain resist adaptation and deserve more weight in decisions.
  • This is not an argument against ambition — it's an argument for anchoring satisfaction in process, mastery, and relationships rather than any single external outcome.
  • Variety, savoring, experiences over possessions, and spending on others all measurably slow adaptation and extend the well-being benefit of a positive change.
  • Weight decisions accordingly: discount the promised happiness boost of material upgrades, and take chronic daily frictions (commute, noise, stress) more seriously than they typically get credit for.
